\begin{abstract}
Localized computation at low temperature could produce technosignatures
that are faint in the mid-infrared but detectable in sufficiently deep
far-infrared or submillimeter observations. We investigate this conditional
hypothesis with two complementary, phenomenological models: a four-state
stochastic cellular automaton and an idealized thermal radiator. The
automaton illustrates how prescribed differences in persistence and
expansion can maintain a predominantly non-expanding population; its
parameters are not calibrated to extraterrestrial civilizations. Mean
occupancies are similar under synchronous and random-sequential updating
for the two parameter sets examined, and isolated expansion lineages
started from a single site all died out within 500 steps. For a
specified irreversible erasure rate of $10^{40}$ bits s$^{-1}$, saturating
the Landauer bound at 10 K gives $9.57\times10^{17}$ W and a total emitting
area of $1.69\times10^{21}$ m$^2$. At 10 pc the corresponding flux densities
are 0.0993, 29.8, 82.3, and 64.7 $\ujy$ at 100, 235, 450, and
850 $\mu$m, respectively. The $S_\nu$ maximum lies near 510 $\mu$m,
outside a nominal 30--300 $\mu$m far-infrared interval. Exact Planck
spectra, rather than Rayleigh--Jeans slopes, are required in these bands.
The 235 $\mu$m benchmark at 10 pc is about 1500 times fainter than the
classical PRIMAger confusion limit, so ordinary single-band blind
extraction is not supported. We give conditional sensitivity
requirements and distinguish a heuristic
Galactic luminosity budget from an empirical foreground constraint.
These models define testable thermal source populations, but neither
establish a universal evolutionary outcome nor identify a unique
resolution of the Fermi paradox.
\end{abstract}

\section{Motivation and scope}
Waste-heat searches test particular assumptions about technological energy
use \cite{Dyson1960,Wright2014}. Their interpretation depends on luminosity,
temperature, geometry, abundance, and selection effects. The absence of a
detected technosignature does not by itself establish either biological
rarity or a preferred developmental trajectory. Inward development and
computational concentration have been proposed as alternatives to extensive
territorial growth \cite{Smart2012}; they remain hypotheses about behavior,
not consequences of thermodynamics alone.

Here, ``inward'' means localized, non-expanding activity. It does not imply
subatomic substrates, disappearance from the observable universe, or
planet-sized cooling infrastructure. ``Cloaking'' denotes reduced
detectability for specified surveys, not intentional concealment or an
absence of radiation. We use 10--30 K as an illustrative radiator
temperature range. We do not derive that range from an optimization of
capital cost, radiator mass, heat transport, radiation exposure, or
computational speed.

Two calculations are kept distinct. The spatial model explores assumed
population rules on an abstract lattice. The radiative model predicts the
spectrum of a specified heat source. There is no calibrated mapping from
lattice sites to stars, or from a population state to an erasure rate.
Connecting the two requires additional demographic and engineering
assumptions. This separation is essential when interpreting a simulated
fraction as a possible Galactic abundance.

\section{A stochastic spatial population model}
\subsection{States and update rules}
Consider a periodic $60\times60$ square lattice with eight Moore
neighbors per site, including diagonal neighbors. Its four states are
empty ($E$), inward ($I$), expanding ($X$), and inactive remnant ($R$).
This is a synchronous stochastic cellular automaton, not a Moran
birth--death process: active population size is variable and no
fitness-proportional replacement event is specified. Neither the lattice
spacing nor its discrete timestep is assigned a physical scale.

Each update reads an unchanged snapshot. The rules are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item An empty site proposes inward emergence with probability $b$.
\item An inward site changes to expanding with probability $m$;
otherwise it disappears with conditional probability $\delta_I$.
\item An expanding site attempts one launch with probability $p$,
selects one of its eight neighbors uniformly, and arrives with
probability $1-s$. An arrival at an old empty site proposes a new
expanding site. An arrival at an occupied site triggers a conflict
with probability $u$, making the source a remnant and, if the target
was expanding, also making that target a remnant. An inward target
is unchanged. This protective asymmetry is an explicit assumption.
\item Each old expanding site independently becomes a remnant with
probability $\delta_X$, and each old remnant disappears with
probability $\delta_R$.
\end{enumerate}
All proposals are resolved together. Colonization overrides simultaneous
spontaneous emergence at an old empty target; any remnant proposal
overrides survival. Multiple identical proposals are merged. An old
expanding site can launch in its final timestep, including if it is
destroyed by an incoming conflict. Newly created sites cannot act until
the next timestep. These conventions remove an implicit traversal-order
dependence in the first implementation and define a particular revised
model; alternative simultaneous-event conventions could change outcomes.

\subsection{Balance relations and limits of a reproduction estimate}
Write the mean site fractions as $e,i,x,r$, with $e+i+x+r=1$,
and define $q=p(1-s)$. Ignoring blocking and contact deaths gives an
upper bound on the expected number of direct colonies produced by one
expanding site over its lifetime:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal R_{\rm upper}=\frac{q}{\delta_X}.
\label{eq:upper}
\end{equation}
This follows from one launch opportunity per step and a geometric lifetime
of mean $1/\delta_X$, including the final step. Blocking, repeated
targets, and conflict can only reduce direct reproduction. For the
baseline $\mathcal R_{\rm upper}=0.70$. Thus extinction of an isolated
expansion lineage is already favored by the prescribed launch and death
probabilities; it does not require a derived governance failure.
For the higher-expansion case the bound is 1.90, so the same argument
no longer establishes subcritical lineage growth.

A homogeneous, approximate estimate that includes only a source's own
outgoing conflict is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal R_{\rm mf}\simeq
\frac{q e}{\delta_X+(1-\delta_X)q u(1-e)}.
\label{eq:mf}
\end{equation}
It is not an exact spatial reproduction number: neighbor correlations,
incoming attacks, repeated colonization attempts, and simultaneous
events matter. In particular, a conflict probability conditional on an
arrival must be multiplied by the arrival probability $q$; it cannot
simply be added as $uP(\mathrm{contact})$ if contact denotes only neighbor
occupancy. We do not assign the earlier unsupported values 0.42 or 0.62
to this process.

Let $\ell=m+(1-m)\delta_I$ and let $c_t$ be the conditional probability
that an old empty site receives at least one successful colony proposal
at step $t$. Independence of the emergence draw gives the exact
ensemble-mean relation
\begin{equation}
i_{t+1}-i_t=b e_t(1-c_t)-\ell i_t.
\label{eq:balance}
\end{equation}
At stationarity this can be rearranged as
\begin{equation}
i_* = \frac{b(1-c_*)}{\ell+b(1-c_*)}(1-x_*-r_*).
\label{eq:equilibrium}
\end{equation}
Neglecting incoming colonies, expanding sites, and remnants yields
$i_*\simeq b/(b+\ell)=0.14287$, an approximate upper envelope,
not a parameter-independent prediction. Varying $\ell/b$ from 0.1
to 100 gives 90.9\% to 0.99\% in this reduced relation, not
50\% to 1\%.

\subsection{Update scheme, correlations, and lineage lifetimes}
To check that the synchronous convention does not drive the occupancies,
we also simulated random-sequential updating with the same local
probabilities. One asynchronous Monte Carlo sweep (MCS) consists of
$N=3600$ independent uniform site selections with replacement; the
selected site applies its local rule to the current lattice. This is a
discrete-time chain, not a continuous-time or Gillespie model.
Selections that cannot change any state are skipped exactly by sampling
geometric waiting numbers, and the accelerated implementation was
checked against a direct implementation and an analytic two-state
expectation. Protocols match Table~\ref{tab:dynamics}: 20,000 steps
or MCS, seeds 0--9, averages over the second half

\section{Thermal emission of a specified computing source}
\subsection{Erasure, reservoir temperature, and radiator area}
For erasure of unbiased, uncorrelated classical bits into a reservoir at
temperature $\Tb$, the Landauer bound is
\cite{Landauer1961,Bennett1973}
\begin{equation}
P_{\rm comp}\geq\Iirr k_B\Tb\ln2.
\end{equation}
Here $\Iirr$ counts irreversibly erased bits per second, not logical
operations, stored bits, or an architecture-independent computational
capacity. Correlations and biased input distributions require an
entropy-based formulation. We parameterize the chosen benchmark by
\begin{equation}
P_{\rm comp}=\eta\Iirr k_B\Tb\ln2,\qquad\eta\geq1.
\end{equation}
The model ignores other power uses and takes $\eta=1$ and
$\Tb=\Trad=T$. In a physical device the computing reservoir and
radiator need not have identical temperatures. Finite heat transfer,
refrigeration, error correction, and power conversion can increase the
total heat load. Equality is a normalization to an ideal lower bound,
not a claim that finite-rate computing achieves it.

For a blackbody radiator with total emitting area $A$ in a negligible
incident radiation field,
\begin{equation}
A=\frac{P_{\rm comp}}{\sigma T^4}
 =\frac{\eta\Iirr k_B\ln2}{\sigma T^3}.
\label{eq:area}
\end{equation}
At $\Iirr=10^{40}$ bits s$^{-1}$ and $T=10$ K,
\begin{align}
P_{\rm comp}&=9.56993\times10^{17}\ {\rm W},\\
A&=1.68771\times10^{21}\ {\rm m^2}.
\end{align}
The equivalent one-face disk radius is $\sqrt{A/\pi}=0.15493$ AU,
or $2.32\times10^7$ km. For a spherical outer surface,
$R=\sqrt{A/(4\pi)}=0.07747$ AU; a disk emitting on both faces has
radius $\sqrt{A/(2\pi)}$. These are different geometries.
Cooling infrastructure is therefore substantial even if the processor
is spatially compact. At fixed erasure rate and $\eta$, lowering $T$
reduces power linearly but increases area as $T^{-3}$.

In an isotropic blackbody background at $T_{\rm bg}$ the net cooling
balance becomes $P_{\rm comp}=A\sigma(T^4-T_{\rm bg}^4)$.
For $T_{\rm bg}=2.725$ K, the required 10 K area increases by about
0.55\%. Stellar irradiation is potentially much larger. An unshielded
blackbody sphere near a Sun-like star has an equilibrium temperature
of approximately $278\,{\rm K}(r/{\rm AU})^{-1/2}$ even without internal
heating; reaching 10 K would require a distance of order 770 AU under
those assumptions. Shielding or another environment is therefore
required for a cold radiator near a star. We do not specify its energy
source, mass, material emissivity, or engineering feasibility.

More generally, the steady-state balance of a radiator with
spectral emissivity $\epsilon_\nu(\hat k)$ is
\begin{multline}
P_{\rm comp}+P_{\rm aux}
+\int\!d\nu\!\int\!d\Omega\,\epsilon_\nu A_\perp I^{\rm in}_\nu \\
=\int\!d\nu\!\int_A\!dA\!\int_{2\pi}\!d\Omega\,
\epsilon_\nu\cos\theta\,B_\nu(T),
\end{multline}
where $P_{\rm aux}$ collects power conversion, refrigeration, and other
non-computational loads, $I^{\rm in}_\nu$ is the direction-dependent
incident intensity, and $A_\perp$ is the projected area facing each
incident direction (absorptivity equals emissivity by Kirchhoff's law).
The benchmark sets $P_{\rm aux}=0$, $I^{\rm in}_\nu=0$, and
$\epsilon_\nu=1$. Each relaxation increases the required area or changes
the spectrum. The total emitting area also does not imply a single rigid
structure; it could be distributed across a swarm. If such a system is
spatially extended compared with the instrument beam, the point-source
forecasts below do not apply.

\subsection{Limits of arguments from reversible computation}
Logical reversibility can reduce the number of erasures associated with
a computation \cite{Bennett1973}. Error correction and environmental
interactions can generate heat, but their rates depend on hardware,
noise, control, and protocol. Neither a universal dissipation law
$P\propto v^2$ nor a universal residual erasure rate of $10^{40}$ bits
s$^{-1}$ follows from these considerations. The conversion of gross
operations to erased bits requires an explicitly defined architecture.
The quantum speed limit bounds dynamical evolution for a specified
energy above the ground state \cite{Margolus1998}; it does not fix that
conversion or establish that the selected benchmark approaches a
planetary computer's capacity. We therefore treat $\Iirr$ as a free
parameter, and make no claim of a nonzero universal heat floor for all
possible intelligences.

\section{Spectral predictions and geometric conventions}
For an unresolved source with isotropic total luminosity, each emitting
surface element contributes a hemispheric blackbody exitance $\pi B_\nu$.
Consequently,
\begin{align}
L_\nu &= A\pi B_\nu(T),\\
S_\nu &= \frac{L_\nu}{4\pi d^2}
       =\frac{A}{4d^2}\frac{2h\nu^3/c^2}{\exp(h\nu/k_BT)-1}.
\label{eq:flux}
\end{align}
This normalization integrates to $P_{\rm comp}/(4\pi d^2)$.
A planar radiator has an inclination-dependent flux; its equivalent
area radius must not be inserted as a spherical radius. If the net
background-cooling model is used, the background-subtracted spectrum
has $B_\nu(T)-B_\nu(T_{\rm bg})$ in Eq.~\ref{eq:flux}.
All tabulated benchmarks below instead use $T_{\rm bg}=0$.

The peak of $B_\lambda$ occurs at $\lambda T=2897.77\ \mu$m K,

A claim about
``the peak'' must state the spectral convention. The thermal signatures
span the far-infrared and submillimeter; they do not all peak inside
30--300 $\mu$m. At 28 $\mu$m the 10 and 30 K fluxes are only
$3.87\times10^{-16}$ and $0.0108\ \ujy$ under this normalization.
This short-wavelength attenuation is \emph{Wien suppression}, not
Rayleigh--Jeans suppression.

\subsection{Exact slopes and astrophysical degeneracies}
An optically thin, single-temperature dust model may be written
$S_\nu\propto\nu^\beta B_\nu(T_d)$ \cite{Hildebrand1983}. Its
local spectral index is
\begin{equation}
\alpha(\nu)=3+\beta-\frac{x}{1-\exp(-x)},
\quad x=\frac{h\nu}{k_BT_d}.
\end{equation}
Only for $x\ll1$ does $\alpha$ approach $2+\beta$.
For two bands at $\lambda_1<\lambda_2$, the measured index is instead
\begin{equation}
\alpha_{12}=\frac{\ln(S_{\nu_1}/S_{\nu_2})}
 {\ln(\nu_1/\nu_2)}.
\end{equation}
Between 450 and 850 $\mu$m, a pure blackbody has
$\alpha_{12}=0.380,1.007,1.553$ at 10, 15, and 30 K. A 15 K,
$\beta=1.7$ optically thin template gives 2.707; the same template
at 10 K gives 2.080. Consequently, even optically thin dust can satisfy
$\alpha<2.5$. Optically thick dust can approach a blackbody, and
temperature mixtures, grain growth, and redshift broaden the possible
spectra. Engineered surfaces need not have $\beta=0$ either. A
blackbody-like continuum is therefore a candidate-selection feature,
not proof of artificiality.

\section{Observational forecasts as sensitivity requirements}
\subsection{Current and future far-infrared observations}
Confusion from unresolved sources must be distinguished from instrumental
thermal noise. For example, SPIRE confusion estimates of 5.8, 6.3, and
6.8 mJy beam$^{-1}$ at 250, 350, and 500 $\mu$m describe sky fluctuations,
not a universal noise floor caused by Herschel's mirror
\cite{Nguyen2010}. Our benchmark sources are much fainter. This comparison
does not characterize every targeted, prior-assisted, or time-domain
analysis of archival data.

The relevant planned PRIMA camera is \emph{PRIMAger}, not GERONIMO.
Its 2025 design paper gives 24--84 $\mu$m hyperspectral coverage and
four broad polarimetric bands centered at 92, 126, 183, and 235 $\mu$m,
with band edges extending to 264 $\mu$m \cite{Ciesla2025}.
NASA selected PRIMA to advance to Phase B on 23 September 2026
\cite{NASA2026}; it is not an operating observatory. We use those
published band centers as reference wavelengths, not final flight
response functions. Bandpass integration and color corrections are
especially important on a 10 K spectrum's Wien side.

Published mapping requirements are not equivalent to a universal
10--15 $\ujy$ point-source RMS in an unspecified integration. Indeed,
the 2025 instrument paper's Table 2 note specifies a 10-hour,
one-square-degree map, whereas adjacent prose refers to a one-hour
reference. We do not extrapolate that inconsistent normalization into
an exposure prediction. Cooling a telescope reduces its own thermal
background but does not remove source confusion. A validated observing
mode, sky field, response curve, and noise model are needed before
claiming a detection time or a survey yield.

\subsection{Source confusion at 235 $\mu$m}
For the 235 $\mu$m PRIMAger band, Donnellan et al.\ estimate a classical
confusion limit, defined as five times the confusion noise, of 46 mJy
\cite{Donnellan2024}. The 10 K benchmark at 10 pc gives 0.02982 mJy,
about 1500 times fainter. Ordinary single-band blind extraction
therefore cannot detect this source at 10 pc. Applying the same
threshold to Eq.~\ref{eq:horizon} gives 0.2546 pc (about
52,500 AU); this is a simple threshold conversion, not a measured
search completeness. Hyperspectral and prior-based deblending can reach
below the classical limit \cite{Donnellan2024}, but the depth achievable
for a particular target requires injection--recovery and false-positive
tests in realistic simulated maps. The $10\ \ujy$ RMS in
Table~\ref{tab:horizon} is therefore a requirement for comparison, not
an achieved sensitivity.

The 2025 design gives a 235 $\mu$m beam FWHM of about 27.9$''$
\cite{Ciesla2025}. The value of about 33$''$ sometimes quoted for a
1.8 m aperture is the first dark-ring radius $1.22\lambda/D$ of an ideal
Airy pattern, not the FWHM.

\subsection{Outer Solar System versus 10 pc}
Two search regimes should be separated. A source in the outer Solar
System at 10,000 AU has an annual parallax of $20.6''$, comparable to
the beam, so multi-epoch differencing can separate it from static
confusion. At 10 pc the parallax is $0.1''$, far smaller than the beam.
When a source moves by much less than a beam between epochs, the
difference map retains only a small fraction of its flux, and
differencing does not escape confusion. The horizons above apply to
neither regime without a modeled observing strategy.

\subsection{Conditional detection horizons}
For total effective RMS $\sigma_\nu$, threshold $n$, and a fixed
unresolved source model, a conditional horizon is
\begin{equation}
d_{\max}=10\ {\rm pc}
\left[\frac{S_\nu(10\ {\rm pc})}{n\sigma_\nu}\right]^{1/2}.
\label{eq:horizon}
\end{equation}
The illustrative choice $\sigma_\nu=10\ \ujy$ and $n=5$ gives
Table~\ref{tab:horizon}. The same noise is used at every wavelength
solely to expose the source-temperature dependence; it is not a
PRIMA or ALMA performance prediction. With $\eta$ and temperatures
fixed, $d_{\max}\propto\Iirr^{1/2}$.

\subsection{ALMA follow-up and identification}
For independent flux errors and sufficiently high signal-to-noise ratios
$\rho_1,\rho_2$, the two-band statistical uncertainty is
\begin{equation}
\sigma_\alpha\simeq
\frac{\sqrt{\rho_1^{-2}+\rho_2^{-2}}}{|\ln(\nu_1/\nu_2)|}.
\label{eq:alphaerror}
\end{equation}
At 450/850 $\mu$m, $\sigma_\alpha=0.15$ requires
$\rho_1=\rho_2\simeq14.8$ for equal signal-to-noise. For the
10 K benchmark this corresponds to statistical RMS of about
5.55 and 4.36 $\ujy$, respectively. Cross-band calibration errors
must also be propagated, including their covariance; independent
10\% fractional errors alone would give $\sigma_\alpha\simeq0.222$.
No universal 20-minute integration claim follows. An ALMA exposure
estimate needs frequency, bandwidth, array, weather, resolution, and
overheads; the observatory calculator reports on-source time
\cite{ALMAcalculator}.

Candidate follow-up should fit complete Planck or modified-blackbody
spectra and compare astrophysical alternatives using spectroscopy,
polarization, morphology, and repeat astrometry. Identification should
be a model comparison that uses measured sizes, motions, spectral energy
distributions, and line intensities together with their upper limits.
We do not adopt fixed far-infrared-to-optical ratios, outright rejection
of a natural model at $3\sigma$, or a conclusion based only on
non-detection of CO or C$^{18}$O lines. Proper motion or
parallax can establish proximity; association with a star can help
locate a source. Neither common motion nor Keplerian or unusual motion
alone establishes artificiality. Searches may include both isolated
sources and cold excess around cataloged stars; requiring no optical
counterpart would exclude the latter by construction.

\section{A conditional Galactic luminosity budget}
If, additionally, each occupied star hosts one identical node, the
aggregate computing luminosity is
\begin{equation}
L_{\rm nodes}=f_\star N_\star\eta\Iirr k_B\Tb\ln2.
\end{equation}
Here $f_\star$ is an assumed stellar occupancy, not an observed fraction
or a calibrated output of the lattice model. With $f_\star=0.14$,
$N_\star=10^{11}$, $T=15$ K, and the benchmark erasure rate,
\begin{equation}
L_{\rm nodes}=2.01\times10^{28}\ {\rm W}.
\end{equation}
This corresponds to $1.4\times10^{10}$ nodes under the one-node-per-star
assumption. Adopt, solely as an illustrative scale,
$L_{\rm TIR}=10^{10}L_\odot=3.828\times10^{36}$ W and allocate
$\epsilon=10^{-3}$ of it to a hypothetical extra component. Then
\begin{equation}
\dot I_{\mathrm{irr,max}}=\frac{\epsilon L_{\rm TIR}}
 {f_\star N_\star\eta k_B\Tb\ln2}
 =1.90\times10^{45}\ {\rm bits\ s^{-1}}.
\label{eq:budget}
\end{equation}
The benchmark is 5.28 dex below this chosen budget, not 2.4 dex.
The Galactic budget $\epsilon L_{\rm TIR}=3.828\times10^{33}$ W must
be distinguished from its allocation per node,
$\epsilon L_{\rm TIR}/(f_\star N_\star)=2.734\times10^{23}$ W, or
$7.1\times10^{-4}\,L_\odot$. Because the energy per erased bit scales
with $\Tb$, the per-node limit is $1.905\times10^{45}$ bits s$^{-1}$
at 15 K and $1.429\times10^{45}$ bits s$^{-1}$ at 20 K.
For $f_\star=0.50$ at 15 K, the corresponding value is
$5.33\times10^{44}$ bits s$^{-1}$. These values correct arithmetic
within the assumed budget; they do not validate the assumption and are
not limits measured with FIRAS.

The cited FIRAS analysis \cite{Fixsen1996} constrains deviations in the
cosmic microwave background spectrum. It does not establish a generic
upper limit of $10^{-3}L_{\rm TIR}$ on an arbitrary Galactic 15 K
population. A real limit requires a spatial distribution, spectral
templates, foreground separation, masks, bandpasses, and measurement
covariance. For example, the sky intensity in direction $\hat n$ would
depend on
\begin{equation}
I_\nu(\hat n)=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int n_{\rm node}(s\hat n)L_\nu\,ds,
\end{equation}
which must be fitted jointly with natural Galactic and extragalactic
emission. A bolometric luminosity comparison alone cannot replace that
analysis. We therefore label the companion budget plot as conditional,
and remove the claim of a measured COBE/FIRAS exclusion region.

\section{Limitations, reproducibility, and conclusion}
The source model neglects non-blackbody emissivity, temperature
distributions, absorbed starlight, anisotropic emission, and power-system
losses. The population model lacks calibrated timescales, a stellar
spatial distribution, or a causal derivation of behavior. In particular,
the nearly 14\% inward fraction is a consequence of chosen emergence,
loss, and interaction rules; it is not a Galactic abundance estimate.

The accompanying code supplies seeded simulation summaries, the
update-scheme comparison and lineage experiment, monochromatic flux
tables, confusion and budget calculations, and five regenerated
figures. Twenty unit and numerical tests check energy conservation by
integrating the Planck spectrum, limiting behavior, flux scaling,
simultaneous-event precedence, a two-state stationary balance, and the
asynchronous implementation against analytic expectations and a direct
implementation. Numerical examples in this draft use two parameter
sets and ten seeds each. They constitute reproducible examples rather
than a full robustness or percolation study. This standalone manuscript
embeds its numerical tables and bibliography; companion figures can be
used in a subsequent journal layout.

Within its stated assumptions, a cold computing source is faint in the
mid-infrared and can be brighter in far-infrared or submillimeter bands.
Low-temperature computation trades reduced erasure energy against much
larger radiator area. Exact spectral modeling changes both discovery
forecasts and dust discrimination. These observations motivate searches
for specified cold thermal populations with explicit completeness and
foreground models, while leaving their abundance, technological origin,
and relevance to the Fermi paradox open.
